# The Cloven Traveling Salesman: Cycle Covers and the Integrality Gap of Small ATSP Instances ∗

## Alessandro Sosso 1, Ambrogio Maria Bernardelli 2, †, , and Stefano Gualandi 2, 

1Department of Computer Science, Aarhus University, Åbogade 34, Aarhus N, 8200, Denmark 

2Department of Mathematics “F. Casorati”, University of Pavia, via Ferrata 5, 27100 Pavia, Italy 

†Corresponding author: ambrogiomaria.bernardelli@unipv.it 

Abstract 

This work proposes a novel enumeration algorithm for computing the integrality gap of small instances of the subtour elimination formulation for the Asymmetric Traveling Salesman Problem ( atsp ). The core idea is to enumerate pairs of cycle covers that can be filtered and mapped to half-integer vertices of the subtour elimination poly-tope. The two-cycle covers are encoded as lexicographically ordered partitions of n

numbers, with an encoding that prevents the generation of several isomorphic vertices. However, since not every cycle cover pair can be mapped to a vertex of the subtour elimination polytope, we have designed an efficient property-checking procedure to con-trol whether a given point is a vertex of the asymmetric subtour elimination polytope. The proposed approach turns upside down the algorithms presented in the literature that first generate every possible vertex and later filter isomorphic vertices. With our approach, we can replicate state-of-the-art results for n ≤ 9 in a tiny fraction of time, and we compute for the first time the exact integrality gap of half-integer vertices of the asymmetric subtour elimination polytope for n = 10 , 11 , 12 .

Keywords: Asymmetric Traveling Salesman Problem, Integrality Gap, Half-integer vertices, Integer programming

The Traveling Salesman Problem ( tsp ) is a long-established combinatorial optimiza-tion problem consisting of finding the shortest tour visiting every node in a given

Among the several algorithms designed over time to solve the tsp , a key method was developed by Dantzig, Fulkerson, and Johnson [13], who introduced a fundamen-tal formulation of the tsp as an Integer Linear Problem (ILP). Despite considerable achievements in improving the tsp algorithms, the inherent difficulty of finding an ef-ficient polynomial time algorithm led many efforts to be devoted to finding algorithms that could provide approximate solutions in polynomial time, both for the symmet-ric [10, 22] and for the asymmetric case [1, 16, 26, 27, 28]. From an integer programming perspective, a natural source of performance guarantees is the linear relaxation of the 

tsp formulation, obtained by dropping the integrality constraints; we refer to this re-laxation as the Subtour Elimination Problem ( sep ). For minimization problems, the optimal value of the linear relaxation provides a lower bound on the optimal integer value. A standard measure of the quality of this bound is the integrality gap, defined as the ratio between the optimal value of the integer problem and that of its linear relaxation (e.g., see [11]). An upper bound on the integrality gap, together with a polynomial-time procedure that converts a relaxation solution into a feasible tour with the same worst-case guarantee, yields an approximation algorithm with the same ratio. While the integrality gap of the tsp is known to be unbounded in the general case, since instances achieving any arbitrary gap can be constructed [15, S4.2], the computa-tion of the maximum integrality gap when considering only metric weights is an open question, as only lower and upper bounds have been proved. Structural analyses of the subtour LP, such as those by Carr and Vempala [7], have identified families of instances where the gap can be tightly characterized, helping to formalize how the LP behaves across different classes of graphs. For the metric stsp , the integrality gap of the sub-tour LP has long been known to be at most 32 (e.g., see [31, 23]), and no instances with a gap of 43 or greater have been found. Recently, Karlin et al. [20] showed that the integrality gap is strictly less than 32 , giving the first improvement on this bound in decades. In the asymmetric case, only a large upper bound of 22 was known [28], which has recently been improved to 17 [29], while the best lower bound has been discovered by Charikar, Goemans, and Karloff [9] and independently by Elliott-Magwood [15] with a family of weighted graphs with an integrality gap tending to 2, and analogous constructions for unweighted graphs are discussed in [29]. The computation of the integrality gap for small tsp instances (i.e., with less than 15 nodes), is of interest since it might provide useful insights into the structure of the instances yielding a high integrality gap. As the maximal integrality gap cannot be computed directly since it would require computing the gap for infinite instances of the tsp , indirect approaches are to be employed. One general framework for analyzing integrality gaps is the fractional decomposition tree method introduced by Carr et al. [8], which provides theoretical bounds by decomposing fractional solutions. In this work, however, we rely on a more computationally driven strategy, as exhibited in [2, 6, 15, 30], where an auxiliary LP problem, derived from the dual of the sep , is leveraged. Such auxiliary gap problem takes as input a feasible solution to the sep , and by calculating its optimum on all vertices of the sep polytope for a given n, the maximal 

2value Gap n of the integrality gap over all n-nodes tsp instances can be obtained. This approach is not exclusive to the TSP problem, as a similar methodology was recently employed to compute the integrality gap of small metric Steiner Tree problems [3], thereby highlighting the generalizability of the approach. The goal of this work is to revisit and extend the approach presented in [15] to com-pute for the first time the exact Gap n for n = 10 , 11 , 12 , for all half-integer extreme points of atsp , that are those vertices with component values in {0, 1/2, 1}. This choice is motivated by computational evidence, as this type of vertices achieve the highest known integrality gaps for those n for which the exact Gap n has been computed over all extreme points [15, S4]. In particular, when talking about the integrality gap of an extreme point, we refer to the maximum ratio between the optimal value of the integer problem and the optimal value of the linear relaxation, with the additional constraint that the extreme point under consideration is an optimal solution of the relaxation. Notice that in [15] and in the previous work [5], the authors could compute Gap n for 

n ≤ 7 for any type of vertex via exhaustive generation of all the vertices of asep . For 

n = 8 , 9, however, the number of vertices was too large for their extensive computation, and the authors focused on computing the integrality gap of all half-integer extreme points. For n > 9, no form of complete enumeration was computationally practicable, but the authors nonetheless provided lower bounds on the value of Gap n by concen-trating on only a few specific instances. To the best of our knowledge, these constitute the most advanced results to date on the exact computation of the maximum atsp 

integrality gap for half-integer vertices. This paper builds upon the master’s thesis [24], where parts of the structural and algorithmic development appear in more detailed form. The present version refines the methodology and extends the computational results to n = 12 .

Our contributions. This paper develops a novel enumeration framework for the ex-haustive computation of half-integer extreme points of atsp and their integrality gap. Our starting point is a structural observation: every half-integer extreme point can be represented as the average of two edge-disjoint cycle covers (Theorem 2). Here and throughout the paper, by a cycle cover we mean a spanning directed 1-regular sub-graph, and therefore a union of vertex-disjoint directed cycles. Decomposition results for fractional solutions follow from known properties of circulation polytopes (see, e.g., [17, 29]); however, to the best of our knowledge this representation has not been formu-lated explicitly in this vertex-level and algorithmic form. We include a self-contained derivation in Section 2, as it forms the structural foundation of our enumeration pro-cedure. Building on this structure, we design a new generation-and-filtering frame-work that constructs a superset of candidate vertices via pairs of cycle covers, encodes them to remove redundancies, and certifies them through feasibility and extremality checks. Unlike [15], where all vertices are first enumerated and only later analyzed, our approach interleaves structural generation and certification, significantly reducing the effective search space and enabling exact computations for larger instances. Akey component of the pipeline is a novel circuit-based extremality verification proce-dure (Propositions 1 and 2) tailored to the combinatorial structure of the generated candidates, which substantially accelerates the extremality test. Combining these in-gredients, we compute for the first time the integrality gap of all half-integer extreme points for n ≤ 12 , extending previous exact computations and confirming the conjec-tured maximum gap within this class. Compared to earlier exhaustive approaches, 

3our framework achieves these results within a fraction of the previously required com-putation time. Beyond these specific computations, the structural generation and certification techniques introduced here provide new methodological perspectives on studying extreme-point structure, which may be applicable to related polyhedral and combinatorial optimization problems. 

Outline. The outline of this paper is as follows. Section 2 presents the problem of computing the integrality gap of the atsp and derives the structural characterization of half-integer vertices that underpins our enumeration algorithm. Section 3 and Sec-tion 4 detail exhaustive and efficient algorithms for generating all half-integer vertices for a fixed n. In particular, while Section 3 exploits Theorem 2 for generating can-didate points that could represent half-integer vertices, Section 4 presents an efficient procedure for checking whether a given candidate point is a basic feasible solution, leveraging the structure of the problem and designing a novel algorithm. In Section 5, we report our computational results on the complete enumeration of half-integer ver-tices for n ≤ 12 , and the evaluation of the integrality gap of each vertex. We conclude the paper with a perspective on future work.

# 3 Vertex generation 

In this section, we present our routine for the generation of half-integer vertices. First, making use of Theorem 2, we describe an efficient way of encoding cycle covers. We then discuss how the generated cycle covers are filtered to consider only non-isomorphic instances that satisfy the subtour elimination constraints (1d) and are extreme points of 

Pn. We say that two instances are isomorphic if the corresponding graphs are isomor-phic as directed graphs. In what follows, we will also assume that V n = {0, . . . , n − 1}

for simplicity, yet similar results could be obtained for any given set of vertices V n by selecting an ordering of its elements.

We encode any cycle cover by the sequences of vertices that each cycle in the cover visits, as in Figure 3. 10 3.3 Feasibility and extremality checking 

Checking for the violation of the subtour elimination constraint is achieved by directly verifying the satisfaction by x of inequalities (1d) by enumeration. We are then left with checking whether x is also an extreme point of Pn (x ∈ Xn). This is equivalent to asking for x to be a basic solution, that is, a solution for which the number of linearly independent active constraints equals the dimension of the solution space. Computing the matrix rank is a demanding task, and hence we elaborate a faster method to evaluate the rank-maximality of the active constraints. Such circuit ex-tremality algorithm (Proposition 2) is extensively described in the following section. 

# 4 Circuit Extremality Algorithm 

In the present section, we develop a novel algorithm to fasten the check for the ex-tremality of a given candidate half-integer solution. The designed algorithm relies on partitioning the non-zero arcs of x based on the degree constraint vectors and its successive coarsening by the addition of the vectors associated with active subtour elimination constraints. For pure half-integer vertices, the support of x has two incoming and two outgoing arcs for each vertex in V n, or equivalently that exactly two arcs are in the support of every degree constraint vector. The initial partitioning is built by repeatedly starting from an arc in the support not yet visited, and successively taking the one other arc having alternately the same head vertex or the same tail vertex as the previous one (i.e. the other arc in the support of the head’s in-degree or tail’s out-degree constraint vector), until the starting arc is reached. The arcs in each loop are alternately grouped in the same partition, thus forming two partitions ( circuits ) per loop that are dual to one another, and together form a circuit pair .The circuit partitioning is then coarsened by successively taking each active subtour elimination constraint, and considering the two arcs in its support that are also in the support of x: if the two arcs belong to the same circuit, it is merged with its dual to a single partition, and we refer to the circuit and its pair as shorted ; if the two arcs belong to two circuits in different pairs, then each of those circuits is merged with the dual of the other circuit to form a unique circuit pair (shorted if any of the two merged pairs is already shorted). This ‘short-circuiting’ procedure constitutes the core of the circuit extremality algorithm: the vertex x is extremal if and only if at the end of the procedure the partitioning consists of a single shorted circuit.

17 6 Conclusions and future work 

In this paper, we have presented a novel approach to compute the integrality gap of small atsp instances that enabled the computation of the exact integrality gap for half-integer vertices for n = 10 , 11 , 12 . In our algorithm, we first generate pairs of edge-disjoint cycle covers through a lexicographically ordered encoding that filters out several isomorphic instances, and later, we efficiently check a few properties that characterize those cycle covers as a vertex of asep . In future works, we plan to apply our approach to larger values of n and to k-tuple of cycle covers, k ≥ 3, to study different classes of fractional vertices, and to adapt the described approach to the symmetric tsp .

# Appendix B Canonical Form of a Cover-set En-coding 

The standard form of a cover-set encoding is not unique. One may define the canonical form of a cover-set encoding the standard form that achieves the minimum in a lexi-cographic order among all possible standard forms (the comparison between elements of the standard forms is again done lexicographically). By construction, this canonical form is therefore unique for each set-cover encoding. This construction presents two issues. First, producing canonical instances indeed requires exploring all their possible standard translations to check whether the minimum has been reached, obviously an extremely demanding operation. Notice also how it is, in fact, equivalent to gener-ating all standard forms and then checking for their canonicity afterward. Second, counterexamples, as shown in Figure 8, display two different cover sets (even with different partitions) in canonical form that represent two isomorphic vertices. The canonical form is therefore not strong enough to identify the isomorphism class of a cover-set. It is therefore equivalent and better for us to generate all standard encodings, since it leaves us the choice of whether to check afterward for their canonicity or directly for their isomorphism class with faster algorithms. In particular, it is computationally more efficient to just check if a generated in-stance is isomorphic to an instance we already encountered.
