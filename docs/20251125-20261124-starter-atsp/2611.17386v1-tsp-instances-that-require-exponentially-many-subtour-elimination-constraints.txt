Abstract. We construct pathological TSP instances that require expo-nentially many subtour elimination constraints (SECs) in the sense that, if a smaller collection of SECs is imposed, then the Dantzig-Fulkerson-Johnson model returns a disconnected solution costing less than an optimal tour. For asymmetric instances on n vertices, we prove a growth rate of Ω(φn), where φ ≈ 1.618 is the golden ratio. A node-splitting reduc-tion gives metric instances with Ω(φn/ 2) growth. We also give instances in the plane under the Euclidean, Rectilinear, and Maximum metrics that require exponentially many SECs. Concorde struggles on them, with solve times growing exponentially despite tiny integrality gaps.

By this definition, an instance G requires R(G) subtour constraints in the sense that all set families S of cardinality less than R(G) yield cost strictly less than TSP optimal. Vercesi and Buchanan [15] computed R(G) for TSPLIB instances, finding that it is often small. Similarly, some of the most challenging metric TSP instances from the literature, the Rectilinear 3-dimensional instances of Zhong [17], empirically seem to require a quadratic number of SECs, Θ(n2). Still, it remained an open question whether any class of instances requires exponentially many SECs, 2Ω(n).

Our contributions. In this paper, we answer this question in the affirmative: there do exist pathological TSP instances that require exponentially many SECs. Section 2 gives our simplest construction, the Ball Bearing construction, which requires 2n/ 5 SECs and can be embedded in the plane, yielding TSP instances under the Euclidean, Rectilinear, and Maximum metrics that also require 2n/ 5

SECs. Section 3 gives a more complex Fibonacci construction that increases the exponential growth to Ω(φn) for asymmetric TSP and Ω(φn/ 2) for metric TSP, where φ ≈ 1.618 is the golden ratio. Section 4 applies the Concorde solver [1,2] to the constructions. Empirically, solve times grow exponentially despite tiny integrality gaps. This stands in contrast with HardTSPLIB and Rectilinear 3-dimensional instances, which stymie Concorde with their large integrality gaps.

## 3 Fibonacci Construction 

The previous section gave a relatively simple construction based on the Ball Bearing graphs, which allowed us to easily obtain TSP instances in the plane under the Euclidean, Rectilinear, and Maximum metrics. Here, we give a more complex construction. We start with a directed graph, the Fibonacci-Sum digraph FS( k), which, like before, is non-Hamiltonian, but admits exponentially many vertex bipartitions {S, ¯S} that induce Hamiltonian subgraphs. By 1,2-weighting, this gives an ATSP instance that requires on the order of φn ≈ 1.618 n SECs. Then, by applying a node-splitting reduction, similar to that of Jonker and Volgenant [6], which doubles the number of nodes, we obtain a symmetric (indeed metric) instance that requires on the order of φn/ 2 SECs. ## 4 Computational Experiments 

In this section, we assess the empirical hardness of the proposed instances by applying the Concorde TSP solver to them. Experiments were conducted on the fat partition of a shared HPC cluster. Each node has two Intel Xeon E5-2650 v3 CPUs (2.30 GHz, 10 cores each, 20 cores total) and 128 GB of RAM. Jobs were limited to 48 hours. The code was written in Python, using PyConcorde [14] (and, as such, QSOpt as an optimizer). The code (including generators for the planar embeddings) and results are available at this link. We consider five classes of TSP instances, including four Ball Bearing construc-tions (1,2-TSP, Euclidean, Maximum, Rectilinear) and one Fibonacci construction (1,2,3-TSP) for different values of n. We apply Concorde to each instance using 10 different seeds and plot the median solve time and interquartile range in Fig. 5. For the Fibonacci construction, the job was killed when k > 20 (n > 44 ) due to a memory issue; all other runs either solved to optimality or exceeded the time limit. Running times are consistent with many other hard instances of similar size in the metric context (e.g., HardTSPLIB [16]). In comparison to the tetrahedron instances of Hougardy and Zhong [5], the Euclidean embedding of the Ball Bearing construction (scaled by 1000) gives results that are two orders of magnitude slower (for 70 nodes >10,000s here versus ≤ 100s in Fig. 12 and 13 of [5]), leading to the “hardest” Euclidean instances currently available in the literature. Fig. 5d and 5e give runtimes for the Maximum and Rectilinear embeddings. The Rectilinear times are faster than those reported by Zhong [17] but are still much slower than those of TSPLIB instances. We remark that the proposed instances are not hard in the usual computational complexity sense. As we saw, the 1,2-weighting of the Ball Bearing construction has TSP( G) = n + 1 , and a matching lower bound is achieved by exploiting the fact that BB( k) is non-Hamiltonian and adding the cut x(E(BB( k))) ≤ n − 1 to the LP relaxation over the degree-two constraints and 0,1-bounds. This specialized “hack” would speed up Concorde but is not algorithmically satisfying. 

## 5 Conclusion and Future Work 

This paper constructs pathological TSP instances that require exponentially many subtour elimination constraints, with growth rates of at least 2n/ 5 for instances in the plane under the Euclidean, Maximum, and Rectilinear metrics, and growth rates of φn for 1,2-ATSP and φn/ 2 for metric TSP. Future work may consider questions like: What is the expected number of required SECs for randomly-generated TSP instances (e.g., in a unit square) or for “typical” instances (however defined)? Is the growth polynomial or exponential? Is the φn growth in SECs for ATSP optimal, or do there exist instances with faster growth rate?
