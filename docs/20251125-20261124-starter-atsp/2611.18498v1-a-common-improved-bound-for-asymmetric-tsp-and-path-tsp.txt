# A Common Improved Bound for Asymmetric TSP and Path TSP 

Mohammad Hajiaghayi Changki Yun University of Maryland, College Park 

hajiagha@umd.edu ckyun@umd.edu 

Abstract 

We give polynomial-time ( R∗ + ε)-approximation algorithms for asymmetric TSP and asym-metric path TSP, for every fixed ε > 0, where R∗ = 6 + √2 + √15 + 6 √2 ≈ 12 .2604. Both guarantees hold relative to the corresponding subtour relaxations, whose integrality ratios are therefore at most R∗. This improves the previous guarantees of below 14 .7 for asymmetric TSP and 15 for asymmetric path TSP [Vygen, 2026]. Our algorithms strengthen subtour covers and recursive augmentation in the laminar framework [Svensson, Tarnawski, Végh, JACM’20; Traub, Vygen, 2025]. The recursive analysis retains the paths through contracted regions and accounts jointly for their cost and the dual mass they visit. We also improve the reduction from asymmetric path TSP to vertebrate pairs [Traub, Vygen, 2025] by comparing two initial walks with complementary completion bounds. Applied to Vygen’s existing vertebrate-pair algorithm, this reduction alone improves the path factor from 15 to below 14 .7, matching his asymmetric TSP guarantee. 

# 1 Introduction 

The asymmetric traveling salesman problem (ATSP) asks for a minimum-cost tour visiting every vertex of a directed graph with nonnegative arc costs. In asymmetric path TSP, the walk must start at a given vertex s and end at a given distinct vertex t. We allow repeated vertices and arcs; on a complete directed metric, shortcutting gives a Hamiltonian tour or an ( s, t )-path. The first constant-factor approximation for ATSP was obtained by Svensson, Tarnawski, and Végh [STV20]. Their laminar framework was subsequently improved by Traub and Vygen [TV22, TV25] and by Vygen [Vyg26], who gives a tour factor below 14 .7 and an ( s, t )-path factor of 15. We improve both guarantees to a common factor. Let LP( G, c ) and LP s,t (G, c ) denote the optima of the standard tour and path subtour relaxations, defined in Section 3, and set

# 2 Technical overview 

The tour improvement comes from accounting for a route through a contracted region together with the cost of completing that region. We first recall why the existing algorithms use this recursive representation. We then explain how retaining the actual routes improves the recursive analysis, how the stronger subtour cover pays for the connections, and how the resulting augmentation guarantee transfers to ( s, t )-paths. 

2.1 The recursive framework 

The subtour LP supplies a fractional solution and a lower bound against which to charge the algorithm. To make this lower bound useful locally, Svensson, Tarnawski, and Végh [STV20] reduce ATSP to instances whose arc costs are represented by weighted cuts. The sets defining these cuts form a laminar family : any two are nested or disjoint. The reduction preserves the LP-relative approximation factor [TV25, Theorem 7.4]. We refer to it as the laminar reduction . After this reduction, an arc costs the sum of the weights of the cuts it crosses. These cuts are tight for the supplied fractional solution, so each contributes twice its weight to the LP value. We call this contribution its dual mass . The nested sets can now serve as regions for a recursive algorithm, with an explicit share of the lower bound assigned to each region. The recursion starts with a connected Eulerian multigraph, called a partial backbone , and extends it until it spans the graph. Within a region W , it contracts the inclusion-maximal proper positive-weight laminar sets missed by the partial backbone. The contracted graph is a simpler instance in which these missed regions are represented by vertices. A solution on this quotient is lifted by replacing every visit to a contracted vertex by a path between its original entry and exit. The contracted regions are then completed recursively. This architecture is inherited from Svensson, Tarnawski, and Végh [STV20, Section 9.2] and Traub and Vygen [TV25, Algorithm 7.8 and Lemma 7.13]. The quotient is where Svensson’s algorithm is used: the image of the backbone meets every remaining nonsingleton laminar set, giving a vertebrate pair. On such an instance, the algorithm maintains a local Eulerian solution outside the backbone. Here local means that an arc crosses no nonsingleton laminar cut. If yv denotes the weight of the singleton cut at v, a local arc uv has cost 

yu + yv. A simple local circuit C therefore costs ∑

A subtour cover supplies connections between the 3current components and to the backbone. Its components meeting the backbone may use nonlocal arcs. Vygen [Vyg26, Sections 2–4] improves the initialization and charging analysis within this framework. 

2.2 Retaining passage costs and recursive progress 

In a recursive call, a quotient vertex represents an entire laminar region. Its entry and exit determine which internal path must be restored when the solution is lifted. For each endpoint pair, the reduction fixes one of the nice paths of Traub and Vygen [TV25, Lemma 7.5]: it lies in the smallest laminar set containing its endpoints (allowing the whole graph) and crosses each laminar boundary at most once in each direction. The basic recursive reduction assigns a fixed charge to the quotient vertex large enough to pay for every such path. Our extension of Svensson’s algorithm retains the actual entry, exit, and path of each selected passage instead. This information links the cost of lifting to progress within the region. The nice-path iden-tity [TV25, Lemma 7.6] equates the path cost, together with its entry and exit contributions, to the dual mass of the laminar sets it visits. We call this visited mass its coverage . A passage of small coverage is cheap but leaves more mass for recursive completion; a passage of large coverage is more expensive but can reduce the completion cost. Our extension of the initialization and charging analysis bounds the total cost of the retained passages while keeping this coverage available to the recursive analysis. Coverage must belong to a connected object from which recursion can continue. Quotient connectivity alone does not provide such an object: two circuits meeting at one contracted vertex may lift to disjoint closed walks. For each contracted region L, we select a retained passage of maximum coverage. When processing L, the whole current connected Eulerian component containing that passage is a possible backbone BL; it may extend outside L. Its coverage is certified before the recursive call. Augmenting BL with arcs inside L to visit every vertex of L then joins all other lifted components meeting L. 2.3 Paying for subtour-cover repairs 

To apply the subtour-cover algorithm inside a recursive region, we first need a fractional comparison circulation there. Restricting the original flow to the region can destroy balance. We choose and price internal return paths to restore it, while retaining the attachment information needed when the quotient solution is lifted (Section 4). This construction also accounts for the fresh backbone choices used by the recursion. The subtour cover used by Vygen rounds a modified fractional circulation and then repairs the entry–exit pairing chosen by rounding. Its cost bound pays for these repairs through an additional singleton-mass term [TV25, Theorem 8.24]. We include the repair prices in the rounding objective and obtain 

c(F ) ≤ 2cTx, 

where F is the cover and x is the supplied fractional comparison circulation. The difficulty is that rounding may choose a different pairing from the fractional one. We price all possible repair pairings and pay for them with fractional paths and cycles removed before rounding. This preserves the locality, degree bounds, and backbone visits required by Svensson’s algorithm and by recursive lifting (Theorem 5.1). The stronger cover reduces the cost of connecting quotient components; passage-dependent accounting reduces the cost of completing their contracted regions. The tour analysis combines these two improvements. 

2.4 From augmentation to (s, t )-paths 

The augmentation guarantee keeps information that a complete-tour factor does not express: the cost of completion decreases with the mass already covered by the partial solution. Our transfer uses this information to compare two initial walks before augmentation. The standard normalization of the path LP supplies two inexpensive ( s, t )-paths in the support of its fractional solution [TV25, Lemmas 9.9, 9.10, and 9.16]. One initial walk preserves both path sections within every strongly connected component. The other visits a maximum-coverage path from the fixed nice-path family in each component and connects it to the entry and exit. The component maximum coverages bound the added connector costs in the first construction; in the second, they also certify mass that no longer needs completion. The first completion bound increases with their sum, whereas the second decreases with it. Keeping this shared quantity in both bounds and selecting the better construction avoids the loss incurred by bounding each one separately. The algorithm completes only the selected walk, with one augmentation call per component. To apply Eulerian augmentation, it closes the walk by one auxiliary return from t to s. The component augmentations use only internal arcs, so removing this return from the resulting Euler tour gives a spanning ( s, t )-walk. Section 7.2 gives the two constructions and proves the strengthened reduction to vertebrate pairs, then applies the same analysis to our recursive augmentation.

3.3 Algorithmic primitives 

We use integral bounded minimum-cost circulation, including ordinary vertex gates, and exact rational linear programming from separation. Finite integral lower and upper arc bounds ensure an integral optimum; cost coefficients may be signed. Cut constraints below are separated by directed minimum cuts. Integral circulation follows from [TV25, Theorem 3.11 and Corollary 3.12]; optimization from separation follows from [TV25, Theorem 2.10] and [GLS88]. We use only the polynomial-time rational version. The exchange arguments in Section 5 use the following consequence of optimization from separation. 

Lemma 3.4 (Relative-interior point) . For a nonempty rational polytope with polynomially bounded rational data and a polynomial-time separation oracle, a rational relative-interior point can be computed in polynomial time. Every valid linear inequality tight there is an equality on the entire polytope. Proof. Starting with a feasible point, find the affine hull by optimizing basis functionals orthogonal to the affine span of the points found so far. A nonconstant functional supplies another feasible point outside that span. After at most the dimension many extensions, the collected points span the affine hull. Their average lies in no proper face: a valid inequality tight at the average is tight at every collected point, hence on their affine span. 6.4 Polynomial running time and explicit output size 

All transportation problems, witness programs, cut separations, shortest paths, and bounded circulation optimizations use rational data of polynomial encoding length. The relative-interior constructions in Section 5 use polynomially many such optimizations. The initialization potential bounds the number of restarts by a polynomial for fixed δ, γ, θ ; between restarts every update to H

reduces the number of quotient components. We also need a polynomial bound on explicit arc multiplicities. Decompose the original primal circulation into at most |E| simple cycles and truncate every coefficient above one to one. Atruncated cycle crosses no positive tight laminar cut, since otherwise its original contribution to that unit cut would exceed one. Thus truncation preserves every tight cut, its port marginals, and the induced objective. It preserves all other subtour inequalities as well: either no truncated cycle crosses the cut, or one retained unit cycle already supplies its required incoming value. Balance and positive support are unchanged, and now xe ≤ | E|. Perform this normalization before computing the boundary data. The private couplings have unit mass. Auxiliary constructions introduce only polynomially many labeled arcs. Each rounding upper bound is the ceiling of twice a polynomially bounded flow amount, so its integral output has polynomially many arc copies. Each stored nice path has at most 

|V | − 1 arcs. The recursive regions are original laminar sets. Distinct branches are disjoint, and a branch descends strictly, so each laminar set is processed at most once. Only one of the three choices is recursively executed. Inherited backbone arcs are kept by reference, not reinserted. The nonrecursive work of each call adds polynomially many new arcs, independently of inherited multiplicities. Summing over O(|V |) calls proves polynomial total output size and running time. The polynomial degree depends on the fixed approximation parameters.
