# Solving the traveling salesman problem using quantum devices: QUBO and HOBO formulations 

G.V. Shuvalov ∗ E.N. Krivtsova A.V. Kapranov M.A. Remnev 

Abstract 

The traveling salesman problem (TSP) forms the foundation for many logistics and optimization tasks. At the same time, this problem is NP-hard and requires significant com-putational resources. Quantum computing promises to substantially accelerate computations or increase solution accuracy. The TSP can be conveniently mapped onto the quadratic unconstrained binary optimization (QUBO) formulation, which in turn can be solved using variational quantum eigensolver (VQE) and quantum approximate optimization algorithm (QAOA) methods, but this mapping requires a quadratic number of qubits. The higher-order binary optimization (HOBO) formulation requires significantly fewer qubits, but it contains higher-order terms. In our work, we thoroughly investigate both formulations and provide a practical recipe for solving the TSP. We give a comprehensive study and compare in detail QUBO and HOBO formulations. 

Keywords: QUBO, HOBO, TSP, quantum computations, VQE, QAOA 

# 1 Introduction 

The Travelling Salesman Problem (TSP), a quintessential NP-hard challenge in combinatorial optimization, remains a critical testbed for algorithmic innovation because of its wide-ranging applications in logistics, circuit design, and data analysis [1]. Despite its theoretical significance, exact solutions for large-scale instances—those involving hundreds or thousands of cities—are out of reach classical computers, owing to the exponential runtime of the Bellman–Held–Karp algorithm [2] and other dynamical programming algorithms [3]. This computational bottleneck has spurred intense interest in quantum computing [4], espe-cially variational algorithms like the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA) [5] and the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE) [6], which promise to exploit quantum parallelism to obtain approximate solutions. Many optimization and logistics problems – including TSP – can be expressed in Quadratic Unconstrained Binary Optimization (QUBO) framework [7, 8] and solved on a quantum annealer or an universal quantum computer via QAOA or VQE. However, the current Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era imposes stringent constraints: limited qubit counts, circuit depth restrictions, and high error rates [9]. These limitations force a trade-off between problem fidelity and hardware scalability. Traditional formulations, such as the QUBO [10], exacerbate this challenge because they require N 2 qubits for N cities—a prohibitive demand as problem sizes grow [8]. To address this, the Higher-Order Binary Optimization (HOBO) formulation [10] emerges as a viable alternative, reducing qubit requirements to N × log( N ) through binary encoding. Yet this efficiency comes at a cost: HOBO introduces higher-order terms in the Hamiltonian, necessitating complex multi-qubit interactions that strain variational algorithms such as QAOA (and, by extension, VQE). Previous studies have highlighted both the advantages and limitations of HOBO formulations in the quantum-based optimization problem. HOBO naturally encodes global constraints and

In particular, experiments that employed Hamiltonians containing with three-qubit terms compared QAOA on IBM and quantum annealing (QA) on D-Wave. The QA on noisy devices often outperformed QAOA because the latter suffers from error accumulation [14–16]. For QAOA, it is shown that error thresholds must be respected in order to compete with QA on HOBO-encoded tasks, and that topological techniques such as factorization of two-qubit operations can dramatically reduce the number of CNOTs and increase the feasibility of the algorithm [13, 17]. In addition to fundamental models such as factorization, hypergraph problems, logistics, and games [16, 18, 19], several works have described algorithms and landscapes analyses for HOBO and QUBO encodings of routing and traveling salesman problems. For the latter, analytical and numerical implementations QAOA, VQE, and Grover are reported, achieving exact solutions for up to 6 cities and demonstrating that a HOBO encoding can attain the same performance with fewer circuit layers [12, 20]. An efficient two-step algorithm for extracting a tour from the HOBO solution was also proposed. Moreover, it has been observed that qubit-based formulations can be directly mapped onto HOBO circuits [21]. Thus, this body of work underscores the need for further development and systematic evaluation of HOBO approaches, especially for TSP, which is the focus of the present study. In this work, we systematically investigate the solution of the TSP in the HOBO formulation and benchmark it against the QUBO formulation. Both the QAOA and the VQE algorithms are studied. We evaluate two performance metrics: the ratio of the obtained tour cost to the optimal cost, and the percentage of instances for which the exact optimal tour is recovered. In addition, we study how the number of Hamiltonian terms scales with the number of two-qubit gates, thereby quantifying the circuit depth overhead associated with each encoding.

2.2.1 TSP as QUBO 

As mentioned above, the TSP is a well-studied NP-hard combinatorial optimization problem with known approximation results [22]. Formally, TSP is defined as follows: given a weighted graph with n nodes (representing locations), find a cycle such that every node is visited exactly once at each timestep t ∈ { 1, . . . , N } with the minimum sum of weights of edges used (the distances between locations). This is also known as a Hamiltonian cycle (minimum weighted cycle). In order to create the QUBO for TSP, we use a one-hot encoding to denote the index of the node visited [8]. Therefore, the binary variable xit is the indicator of the condition if the i-th city is visited at timestep t. If the condition is true, then xit = 1 , and otherwise xit = 0 . 43 Methods 

To find the minimum energy and the corresponding qubit state of a target Hamiltonian, several approaches can be employed. In this work the minima are obtained with variational quantum algorithms (VQAs) [23], which are executed on universal quantum devices (in our case on noise-free simulators). The structure of a VQA is shown in Fig. 2. A VQA is a hybrid quantum-classical algorithm. The quantum circuit is parametrized by a set of angles θ1, θ 2, θ 3, . . . . After circuit is applied, the resulting wave function depends on these parameters ψ(θ1, θ 2, θ 3, . . . ). Then, qubit measurements are performed, or the expectation value of the Hamiltonian is evaluated. The classical optimizer treats the measured expectation value as a cost function and updates the parameters θi so as minimize it, for example be gradient descent. 

Figure 2: General structure of variational quantum algorithms. In short, a parameter-dependent quantum circuit acts on the qubit register in the initial state, producing a new quantum state from which the energy is calculated. Consequently, the expectation value of the Hamiltonian is a function of the circuit parameters. The quantum circuits employed for the VQA are the QAOA and Hardware-Efficient ansatz for VQE (VQE-HEA). Below we discuss each of them in more detail. QAOA is an algorithm that simulates an adiabatic change of an initial Hamiltonian into a target Hamiltonian [5]. In this situation, theory predicts that the lowest-energy state of the initial Hamiltonian should evolve into the lowest-energy state of the target Hamiltonian (in our case the QUBO or HOBO Hamiltonian). QAOA, in contrast, depends on the form of the problem Hamiltonian, so for QUBO and HOBO formulations the quantum circuit will differ somewhat. The optimizer employed is Adaptive Moment Estimation (ADAM) [24]. Gradients are computed using a second-order finite-difference formula for QAOA and the parameter-shift rule for VQE (since the gate parameters are independent) [25]. The quantum-simulation frameworks used were Yao.jl [26] and NVIDIA CUDA-Q [27]. The first framework was employed for many small-scale runs because of its efficient gradient computation; whereas the latter was used for larger simulations owing its effective utilization of GPU resources. The main metrics used to compare the QUBO and HOBO formulations were: • Approximation ratio (AR) – the ratio of the energy obtained by the algorithm to the globally minimal energy. In the figures we plot the inverse of this quantity (so that the value lies between 0 and 1); larger values indicate better performance. • True percentage (TP) – the percentage of correct answers among 10000 measurements of the quantum circuit. Larger values are better.

# 5 Discussion 

Based on the obtained results, can conclude that HOBO formulation performs more efficiently than the QUBO formulation when solving TSP. This advantage is likely due not only to the reduction in the number of qubits required but primarily to the decrease in the number of non-existent solutions. An additional, important advantage of the HOBO formulation – unlike QUBO – is that it constrains the eigen-energies of the states more tightly. This tighter bound arises both the smaller number of invalid solutions and from the way the Hamiltonian itself is constructed. As a result, VQE and QAOA that employ the HOBO formulation reach energies close to the exact solutions more quickly in terms of circuit depth (and, secondary, in terms of the number of training iterations). From the perspective of the optimization landscape with respect to the variational algorithm parameters, the HOBO Hamiltonian is more intricate. However, because its landscape is much flatter, it is potentially better suited for gradient-based methods: the flatter terrain makes it easier to escape from local minimum, for example by leveraging noise, momentum, or other perturbations. The main drawback remains that the HOBO formulation is rather bulky. However, there exist techniques – such as the one described in reference [17], that can reduce the count of two-qubit gates by order of magnitude, thereby improving the overall accuracy of the HOBO approach. Thus, future research on the HOBO formulation of TSP should primarily focus on adapting it to real quantum hardware. In addition, exploiting QAOA’s ability to efficiently filter out invalid solutions appears to be a promising direction. 

# 6 Conclusion 

The traveling salesman problem is a rather challenging problem to reformulate for quantum devices. In this paper two formulations for universal quantum computers are examined the QUBO formulation and the HOBO formulation. The first one is intrinsically simpler, but it requires a quadratic number of qubits with respect to the number of cities, whereas the second one needs only N ⌈log 2 N ⌉ qubits ( N – the number of cities), which makes it potentially more efficiently stimulable on classical hardware. The main drawback of the second formulation is that, when used naively becomes larger (especially if the quantum algorithm, in some way, requires the Hamiltonian to be embedded inside the circuit). HOBO yields better results than QUBO: its convergences more efficiently toward the exact energy minimum and, as consequence, more frequently returns the correct tours. This advantage is likely related to the flatter landscape of the HOBO formulation with respect to the variational parameters of quantum algorithms. In addition, it contains fewer invalid states and requires fewer qubits.
