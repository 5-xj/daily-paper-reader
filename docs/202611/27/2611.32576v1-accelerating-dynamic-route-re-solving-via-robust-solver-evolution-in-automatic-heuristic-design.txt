Solver Evolution in Automatic Heuristic Design

Abstract—Multi-goal route planning uses spatial data on ser-
vice locations and pairwise travel costs to determine a visiting
order that minimizes total travel cost. This task can be formulated
as the traveling salesman problem (TSP) and has benefited from
large language model-based automatic heuristic design (AHD)
for constructing effective solvers. Despite their effectiveness in
static settings, existing methods exhibit substantial re-solving
inefficiency when location changes, new requests, or request can-
cellations alter the underlying request data during execution. Our
empirical analysis suggests that “static-overfitting” contributes
to this inefficiency, as evolved solvers may repeatedly direct
search toward overlapping local neighborhoods after dynamic
changes. We further find that this “overfitting” is associated
with limitations in existing evolutionary processes, where lower
objective values on some instances coincide with longer solving
times. In this paper, we therefore propose ERDR, a plug-
and-play framework that integrates two strategies into existing
AHD pipelines to guide evolution toward solvers that re-solve
dynamically changed instances faster. Specifically, 1) ERDR
uses validation-guided reselection to reevaluate candidates on
instances excluded from routine evolution, reducing specialization
to fixed training instances. 2) ERDR applies runtime robustness-
augmented evaluation, supplementing the original evaluation
objective of AHD methods with inter-group variability and tail
risk in solving time to discourage costly and uneven search.
Experiments across multiple TSP scales and dynamic scenarios
show that ERDR achieves average re-solving speedups of 2.2×
under guided local search and 4.4× under iterated local search.†
I. INTRODUCTION
Multi-goal route planning is a core computational task in
location-based services such as delivery and field operations. It
uses spatial data describing target locations and travel costs to
determine a visiting order that minimizes travel cost, a task that
can be formulated as the traveling salesman problem (TSP) [1],
[2]. Given the NP-hardness of TSP [3], solving such problems
within practical computational budgets often relies on heuristic
solvers to obtain high-quality routes. However, developing
effective heuristics and configuring them for different problem
settings requires substantial expertise and effort [4]. Built on
Transformer architectures [5], modern large language models
(LLMs) exhibit broad few-shot capabilities [6] and improved
instruction following [7], including program synthesis from

Existing LLM-based AHD methods typically evolve heuris-
tics on static data with fixed node sets and locations. As illus-
trated in Figure 1 (a), the evolved solver plans a tour for the
known service nodes, which the vehicle then follows to serve
them in sequence. During route execution, changes in service
requests update the underlying spatial data through changes to
unvisited service locations, new requests, and request cancella-

tions [12]. As shown in Figure 1 (b), the remaining route must
then be reoptimized from the vehicle’s current location using
the updated information. Such route updates are central to dy-
namic routing and widespread in real-world operations, includ-
ing courier delivery and field maintenance [13]. For example,
tabu search [14] reoptimizes courier routes as new customer
requests arrive while vehicles are in transit. RpALNS [15] uses
parallel adaptive large neighborhood search to update techni-
cian routes as new service requests become known. However,
our investigation with guided local search (GLS, see details
in Section VI-E) shows that directly reused solvers require
substantially more solving time than counterparts evolved on
the changed data. This additional solving cost delays route
updates in response to changing request data. As shown in
Figure 1 (c)–(d), under location changes and request cancella-
tions at 20% tour progress on TSP500, directly re-solving the
tour with the EoH/Hercules-evolved solvers takes, on average,
3.3/2.8 times as long as with their specialized counterparts.
Our analysis in Section IV-A suggests that static overfitting
contributes to the re-solving inefficiency observed above, as
evolution favors heuristics tailored to the structures of static
source instances that may struggle to maintain stable search
behavior after instance changes. During re-solving, the reused
solver repeatedly directs the search toward overlapping local
neighborhoods, incurring additional search cost to obtain
high-quality solutions. To understand how this “overfitting”
emerges during evolution, we identify two critical findings: 1)
improvements in solution quality increasingly concentrate on
a few source instances, allowing aggregate quality to improve
while most instances receive little benefit. 2) these gains arise
mainly on instances with longer solving times, indicating
that quality improvement is coupled with increasingly costly
and uneven search. These findings suggest that existing
evolutionary processes tend to retain solvers whose gains are
confined to a narrow subset of instances and accompanied
by increasingly costly search, contributing to inefficient re-
solving. We will elaborate on specific details in Section IV-B.
We thus introduce ERDR (Efficient Re-solving in Dynamic
Scenarios via Robust Solver Evolution), a plug-and-play
framework that integrates with existing AHD methods. Con-
sidering that validation sets are commonly used in machine
learning to mitigate “overfitting” to training data, ERDR firstly
introduces validation-guided reselection to reduce candidate
specialization to particular instances. Under this mechanism,
the instances are partitioned into disjoint training and valida-
tion groups, with routine evolution conducted on the training
groups and candidates reevaluated on the validation groups
to update the population. ERDR further introduces runtime
robustness-augmented evaluation, which supplements original
evaluation objective of AHD methods with inter-group vari-
ability and tail risk in solving-time. These criteria incorporate
the associated computational costs into evolutionary selection,
encouraging stable and efficient search across instances. These
two strategies form a robust evaluation and selection mech-
anism designed to improve solver transferability and search
stability when guiding tour re-solving in the dynamic scenario.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:
1) We identify substantial re-solving overhead when re-
solving tours in dynamic TSP scenarios: solvers evolved
under static conditions require substantially longer solv-
ing times when reused after instance changes during tour
execution.
2) Our analysis suggests that “static-overfitting” contributes
to this failure and shows that mean-based evaluation
allows solvers with quality gains concentrated on a
narrow subset of instances and increasingly costly search
to remain competitive during evolution.
3) We propose ERDR, a plug-and-play evaluation frame-
work that combines validation-guided reselection to
reduce specialization to fixed training instances with
runtime robustness-augmented evaluation to accelerate
dynamic re-solving.
4) Experiments across multiple dynamic TSP scenarios
show that ERDR can be integrated into different AHD
methods, achieving average tour re-solving speedups of
2.2× under GLS and 4.4× under ILS.
II. RELATED WORK
a) Automatic heuristic design: Classical TSP heuristics
such as guided local search (GLS) [16] and Lin–Kernighan
search [17] rely on expert-designed [18] neighborhoods and
update rules [19]. Hyper-heuristics [20] search over heuristics,
while FunSearch [21] combines LLM-generated code [22]
with
systematic
evaluation
[23].
Recent
AHD
methods
adopt different evolutionary mechanisms [24]. EoH [10] co-
evolves ideas and code, ReEvo [11] uses reflective feedback,
Hercules [25] derives search directions from elite heuristics,
and MEoH [26] applies multi-objective selection. Other
studies broaden exploration through MCTS, joint algorithm-
model evolution, and hindsight and foresight prompting [27]–
[29]. These methods evaluate candidates on fixed instances
and do not examine solver reuse after instance changes.
b) Dynamic Scenarios in TSP: Dynamic routing methods
update routes as new information becomes available during
execution [13]. Existing routes can be adapted to updated
requests and used as starting solutions for further optimization.
TSP reoptimization has been studied under node insertion and
removal, using an optimal tour of the original instance [30].
Insertion-based route planning incorporates newly arriving
requests into existing routes [31]. Other approaches retain
information across changing environments. Explicit memory
stores previous solutions for reuse [32], while ACO-based
methods combine search information with local improve-
ment [33]. A scheme library-based ACO method maintains
precomputed route schemes for recurring or similar node-
location configurations and combines their reuse with 2-opt
local search [34]. These studies adapt routes through manually
designed heuristics and reuse mechanisms. In comparison, our
work focuses on enabling heuristics automatically designed
by LLMs to be efficiently reused when locations or requests
change during route execution.

III. PRELIMINARY
a) LLM-Based Automatic Heuristic Design.: Given a
prompt specifying the task and required function interface, the
LLM iteratively generates or revises candidate heuristics using
selected parents and their evaluation feedback [10], [11]. Each
candidate heuristic is embedded in a fixed search framework to
form a heuristic solver for tour optimization [16]. The resulting
solver is evaluated on a set of instances, and its performance
guides candidate selection and subsequent generation to evolve
effective heuristics.
Formally, let Ds = {xi}N
i=1 denote the source instance set,
where N = 1 corresponds to single-instance evaluation. Given
a candidate heuristic h, the search framework uses h to obtain
a tour for each instance in Ds, and the task-specific evaluation
E(h; Ds) summarizes its performance. At iteration t, let Pt de-
note the current population and Qt the set of newly generated
or revised candidate heuristics. The population is updated as
Pt+1 = SelectM (Pt ∪Qt; E(·; Ds)) ,
(1)
where SelectM retains M candidate heuristics according
to their evaluation results. The selected population and its
evaluation results jointly guide candidate generation in the
next iteration.
b) Dynamic Re-solving.: When location changes, new
requests, or request cancellations alter the set of unserved
requests or their locations during tour execution, the remaining
task must be reoptimized. The executed prefix remains fixed,
while the remaining task requires a path from the current loca-
tion through the updated unvisited nodes to the original starting
node. The search framework starts from a warm-start route,
initializes its search state, and reuses the heuristic previously
evolved on static instances to optimize the remaining task.
Formally, let h⋆denote the heuristic selected after evolution
on the source instance set Ds, πi the execution tour for source
instance xi, and ∆i the change event at tour progress ρ. The
corresponding residual instance is

IV. OBSERVATION
The following analysis first investigates why re-solving
updated instances becomes inefficient and then examines the
key factors in solver evolution that give rise to this behavior.
A. Observation 1. Why Inefficient?
To examine why solving time increases when re-solving
updated instances in Figure 1, we analyze the solver’s search
behavior during this process. We take the best EoH heuristic
evolved on static TSP200 instances and reuse it within GLS to
re-solve the remaining task after 20% tour progress, with new
nodes added at 20% of the unvisited-node count. Figure 2 (a)
shows the edge selections on the original 200-node instance,
while Figure 2 (b) shows the edge selections on the changed
instance, with thicker lines indicating higher selection frequen-
cies. These edges concentrate in a small region, indicating that
the search repeatedly revisits overlapping local neighborhoods.
This behavior suggests that “static-overfitting” may contribute
to re-solving inefficiency, as static evolution biases the
heuristic toward structural patterns in the source instances and
may thereby increase the search effort required to obtain high-
quality solutions after instance changes. The evolved solver
may thus become specialized to the static scenario and struggle
to maintain stable search behavior after instance changes.
B. Observation 2. How “Overfitting” Emerges?
We investigate how “static-overfitting” emerges by tracking
the solvers retained by EoH at different stages of evolution on
TSP200. At different iterations, we evaluate the evolved solver
obtained at each checkpoint on the same 50 training instances.
For each instance, we record the solving time and objective
value. Figure 2 (c)–(e) visualizes the joint distributions
of these two measures across the three checkpoints and
reveals two observations: 1) Across the three checkpoints,
clear quality improvements appear in an increasingly small
subset of training instances, while most instances show

A. Validation-Guided Reselection
Drawing on the train–validation split used to curb over-
fitting in model selection [35], we divide the instances into
disjoint training and validation groups for routine evolution
and reselection, respectively. Given a source instance set
Ds, we randomly partition it into K groups, denoted by
B = {G1, . . . , GK}, and assign them to the training split Btrain
and validation split Bval such that
B = Btrain ∪Bval.

New candidate solvers generated from the current popu-

candidates entering survivor selection on Bval under the same
criteria and use their validation results to select survivors. After
evolution, we select the heuristic with the highest DDS score
on Bval from the final population for dynamic testing.

C. Framework Overview
ERDR is designed as a plug-in framework for solver evolu-
tion, and its overall workflow is illustrated in Figure 3. Starting
from an initial population, the underlying AHD method gen-
erates new candidate solvers through its original evolutionary
operators. ERDR evaluates the expanded candidate pool under
the proposed selection strategy and retains promising solvers
for the next generation. At each population update, candidates
entering survivor selection are evaluated on the validation
instances, and their validation results determine the survivors
before evolution continues.

D. Baselines
We pair four representative AHD methods, EoH [10],
ReEvo [11], Hercules [25], and MEoH [26], with their ERDR
variants, preserving solver generation and evolutionary sched-
ules while adding runtime-robust evaluation and validation-
guided reselection to candidate selection.
E. Search Frameworks and Operators
We evaluate ERDR within two search frameworks, GLS
and iterated local search (ILS), in our experiments. Within
GLS [16], we evolve update_edge_distance to map
the original distance matrix, current tour, and edge-use counts
accumulated during perturbation to modified distances guiding
2-opt and relocate perturbations. A 2-opt move reverses the
segment between two removed tour edges and reconnects
the tour, while relocate reinserts a node elsewhere. Within
ILS [42], we evolve perturb_tour to map the original
distance matrix and current tour to a perturbed tour with the
same node set. We use double-bridge perturbations to cut four
tour edges and reconnect the resulting segments in a different
order. In both frameworks, we improve perturbed tours with
fixed 2-opt and relocate local search under the original
distances. For ILS, we accept a shorter tour and start the next
search iteration from the newly accepted tour as usual.

of 500 iterations and a per-instance time budget of 10 seconds
for both TSP200 and TSP500, stopping when either limit is
reached. We check time before each iteration, allow an ongo-

H. Ablation Study
We compare the complete ERDR with five ablation variants
for Hercules within GLS. w/o Val. removes validation-guided
reselection while retaining the two time criteria, whereas
w/o Time1 retains reselection but removes both criteria.
w/o Time2 replaces inter-group variability and tail risk with
mean per-instance solving time while preserving the original
objectives and validation-guided reselection. w/o σt and w/o
wt further isolate the contributions of inter-group variability
and tail risk, respectively. All variants use the same generation
budget and search settings within each baseline. Table IV
reports mean solving times. Among the evaluated Hercules
ablations, the complete framework consistently achieves
the lowest solving time. Removing both time criteria (w/o
Time1) causes the largest overall increase, indicating their
substantial contribution to reducing search time. Replacing
the two robustness criteria with mean solving time improves
efficiency over w/o Time1 but remains slower than Full across
all scenarios. Removing tail risk leads to a larger overall
increase than removing inter-group variability, suggesting
a greater contribution from the tail-risk criterion under the
evaluated settings. Removing validation-guided reselection
also increases solving time, showing its additional benefit
alongside the time criteria. Larger absolute time savings
are observed under new requests and at early tour progress,
where more nodes remain to be optimized.
I. Boundary Analysis
We examine how the solving-time advantage of ERDR

At each tour-progress level ρ ∈{20%, 50%, 80%}, we vary
change strength within each dynamic scenario separately. We

r1 over {10%, 20%, 30%, 40%, 50%} for new requests, and


r2 over the same ratios for request cancellations, keeping all

J. Parameter Analysis
We examine the sensitivity of ERDR to the training–
validation allocation and validation interval by varying the
training-group count and the number of population updates
between consecutive validation-guided reselections. We vary
one parameter at a time within GLS on TSP500 with new
requests added at a node-addition ratio of 20%, evaluating
EoH+ERDR and Hercules+ERDR at 20%, 50%, and 80% tour
progress.
Figure 5 shows that solving time initially decreases and then
increases as the training-group count increases. Increasing
the training allocation expands the instance coverage of
routine candidate evaluation while leaving fewer instances for
validation-guided reselection. An equal allocation between
training and validation yields the lowest solving time for
both methods across the three progress levels. Validation at
every population update or every two updates yields similar
solving times. Longer intervals increase solving time, with
the largest absolute increases at 20% tour progress, indicating
that frequent validation-guided reselection benefits re-solving
efficiency in this setting.

L. Runtime Stability and Tail Performance
We further examine whether the acceleration achieved by
ERDR is accompanied by reduced variation in search time
across instances and lower costs in slower instance groups
during dynamic re-solving. Following the default experimental
settings, we compare EoH and ReEvo with their ERDR vari-
ants under GLS and ILS on TSP200 and TSP500 at 20% tour
progress. Using the time required by each solver to first match
or improve on the paired common objective defined in Sec-
tion VI-K, we compute inter-group standard deviation σt and
tail mean wt to characterize runtime variability and slow-group
costs, respectively. We also report instance-level standard de-
viation σinst. Results use one evaluation run per configuration.
Figure 6 shows that new requests generally incur greater
inter-group variability and tail costs, consistent with the
increased
search
burden
from
additional
nodes.
ERDR
reduces both metrics in this scenario, mitigating uneven
runtimes and high slow-group costs as the remaining task
expands. Request cancellations generally have lower tail
costs than those observed with new requests, and ERDR
further reduces runtimes in the slower groups. Under location
changes, both metrics also improve with unchanged node
counts, extending the gains to re-solving after spatial changes.
These results indicate that ERDR alleviates uneven runtimes
and high costs in slower instance groups across dynamic
scenarios, contributing to more efficient dynamic re-solving.

O. Case Study
a) Spatial selection patterns.: We use a representative
TSP200 instance as an example to illustrate differences
in penalty-edge selection between the baselines and their
ERDR-enhanced variants within GLS. At 20% tour progress,
we add new requests equal in number to 20% of the unvisited
nodes, and we compare the subsequent re-solving behavior
of the four baselines and their ERDR-enhanced variants. We
record the edges selected for penalization by each evolved
heuristic and aggregate their selection frequencies over
the search. Figure 7 presents these patterns in a common
coordinate layout, with nodes aligned to the EoH baseline
and edge endpoints mapped accordingly while retaining
the original stroke styles. The baseline heuristics repeatedly
select penalty edges within limited regions, although the
degree of concentration varies across backbones. In all four
comparisons, the ERDR-enhanced heuristics cover broader
regions and reduce the dominance of local high-frequency
clusters while retaining backbone-specific selection patterns.
These
observations
suggest
less
spatially
concentrated
penalty-edge selection during re-solving on this instance.
b) Evolved heuristic rules.: We select representative final
heuristics evolved by EoH and EoH+ERDR on TSP500 as
examples to examine their edge-distance update rules. Figure 8
presents the edge-distance update logic embedded in these
final solvers, where edge costs are revised using the current
locally optimal tour and accumulated edge-use counts before
the next search step. EoH computes q_low and q_high
from the full sparse N ×N usage matrix before applying the
multipliers 0.75, 1.10, and 0.97. A sufficiently large frac-
tion of zero entries makes both thresholds zero, so all edges
with positive counts receive the same combined multiplier of
1.10 × 0.97 = 1.067, regardless of their count magnitudes.
EoH+ERDR also selects edges with positive counts, then
multiplies their costs by a random factor calibrated by the ratio
of average_distance to the current edge distance. The
adjustment therefore varies with each edge’s distance relative
to the current tour. Such distance-dependent updates may
help adapt search guidance after instance changes, offering a
possible explanation for the observed time reductions.

VII. CONCLUSION
In this paper, we investigate inefficient re-solving by AHD-
evolved TSP solvers when instances change during route
execution. Our analysis suggests that “static-overfitting” con-
tributes to this inefficiency, showing that evolved solvers
repeatedly direct search toward overlapping local neighbor-
hoods after such changes. We further link this behavior
to evolutionary processes that retain heuristics whose qual-
ity gains concentrate on a few instances and are coupled
with longer solving times. We propose ERDR, a plug-and-
play framework combining validation-guided reselection and
runtime robustness-augmented evaluation. Experiments across
four AHD methods, two search frameworks (GLS and ILS),
multiple TSP scales, and three change types demonstrate that
ERDR reduces tour re-solving time.
